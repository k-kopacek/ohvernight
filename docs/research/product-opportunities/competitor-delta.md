# Competitor delta

Status: analysis for the owner, written 2026-10-07. Not a decision and not a
roadmap change. No web access was used. Source material is
`hermes/c8a-competitor-strategy-mapping.md` and
`hermes/c8b-competitor-strategy-camping.md`, with prices cross-checked
against `hermes/p1-competitive-landscape-and-pricing.md` and
`business/hermes/p18-pricing-and-costs.md`.

Labels: every factual statement about a competitor is **HERMES-SOURCED**
(unverified web research; URL carried where a number is cited). Every
judgement under the eight headings is **INFERENCE** unless marked otherwise.
**UNKNOWN** means the reports do not establish it. Hermes did not walk
through any competitor's signed-in product; it read official pages, terms and
help centres.

## Summary

Read honestly, the two Hermes reports say the same thing seventeen times:
each incumbent is far ahead on its own job, and none of them answers "may I
sleep here?" with a dated statement from the responsible authority. That is
true. What Hermes could not see is that Ohvernight does not answer it today
either: `camping_permission` and `public_access` coverage are `none` in both
regions. So wherever a section below says a competitor does something
"worse than Ohvernight", read "worse than Ohvernight intends to". Today the
delta runs almost entirely one way.

Two patterns recur and are stated once here instead of seventeen times:

- **Nobody will switch.** In no case is there a plausible feature that makes
  a user abandon an incumbent for Ohvernight. Field navigation, offline
  maps, reviews, bookings and alerts all stay where they are. The realistic
  outcomes are "used alongside" or "not used". The "switch" answers below
  describe which part of a planning session could move, not which
  subscription gets cancelled.
- **"Pay for both" is the whole commercial question,** and no report
  contains evidence that anyone would. It is a hypothesis in every row.

"Replace them" is answered because the brief asks. For every national
product the honest answer is that Ohvernight cannot, and should not try.

# Part A — Mapping and navigation (C8A)

## onX (Hunt, Offroad, Backcountry, Fish)

Prices: Backcountry $29.99 to $99.99 a year; Offroad $34.99 to $99.99;
Hunt $34.99 one state to $99.99; Elite monthly $14.99
(https://www.onxmaps.com/backcountry/app/pricing,
https://www.onxmaps.com/offroad/app/pricing,
https://www.onxmaps.com/hunt/app/pricing). Strategic investment from TCV
announced 2025-11-03; a motorised dispersed-camping layer shipped in Offroad
(https://www.onxmaps.com/news).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** Almost everything a user
would notice: national parcel ownership cleaned in-house, offline maps,
GPS, activity-tuned layers, and now dispersed-camping corridors. They are
also the incumbent most candid that ownership is not access; their own
report says road data conveys "road type rather than public access"
(https://www.onxmaps.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/onX_TRCP_West_Federal_Landlocked_Report.pdf).

**What they do worse.** They stop at context. No per-place statement of
what the agency says, when it was read, and what is unresolved. And they
split one weekend across separately billed apps whose markups do not sync.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Parcel-scale land data and offline
delivery, backed by capital and an operation running since 2009
(https://www.onxmaps.com/about). Not contestable.

**What we should not copy.** Per-activity products and paywalled land
layers. Above all, a dispersed-camping *layer*: a coloured corridor is the
exact presentation the trust principles exist to prevent.

**What their users still need another app for.** The actual rule: stay
limits, designated-site-only areas, fire orders, permits. And anything
bookable.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None for navigation. A share of
pre-trip research could move if Ohvernight showed, for a specific Colorado
place, the governing order and its dates beside the overnight option.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** A Colorado access answer
that onX's Elite tier ($99.99) does not give them. This is the hardest "pay
for both" on the list, because the buyer already believes they bought it.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** Nothing Ohvernight can
build. The reverse is the real question: onX is the company most able to add
claim-level provenance for Colorado if it chose to accept the liability.

## Gaia GPS (Outside)

Prices disagree across reports and are shown as found: Premium $59.90 a year
(P1, https://www.gaiagps.com/premium); a publisher article listing $39.99,
current price UNKNOWN (P18,
https://www.outsideonline.com/adventure-travel/advice/best-map-app-strava-gaia-gps-mountain-project-alltrails-caltopo/?scope=anon);
official checkout unreadable, current price UNKNOWN (C8A). Outside+ bundle
$89.99 a year (P18, https://www.outsideonline.com/outsideplus-salesevents/?scope=anon)
against $71.99 first year (P1). More than 250 map sources
(https://help.gaiagps.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003524587-What-maps-are-included-in-Gaia-GPS).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** A layer workspace: MVUM,
public and private land, topo, satellite, all offline, worldwide.

**What they do worse.** It hands the user the raw materials and leaves the
conclusion to them. Attribution is per layer, never per claim. Complaint
mining also shows a product in visible distress under its owner: price
transition, forced logins, failed downloads
(https://www.trustpilot.com/review/gaiagps.com).

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Breadth of licensed sources and the
bundle economics behind them.

**What we should not copy.** The layer catalogue. Gaia itself had to drop
Esri imagery over licensing cost
(https://blog.gaiagps.com/gaia-gps-is-improving-satellite-imagery-saying-goodbye-to-esri-world-imagery/).
A layer count is a cost centre, not a product.

**What their users still need another app for.** Interpretation, rules,
reservations, and a simple place to start.

**What feature could make a user switch.** For planning only: a finished
answer for one place in place of five overlays. Gaia users are the expert
segment, though, and experts enjoy the overlays.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** An evidence packet they can
export and carry into Gaia. Export matters more than anything on-screen.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None. Gaia is more likely to
be weakened by its owner than by a competitor.

## AllTrails

Plus $35.99 a year after a seven-day trial (https://www.alltrails.com/plans);
Peak price UNKNOWN in every report (P1's executive summary places Peak in an
$80 to $100 band while its own table says the price was not exposed; treat
as unestablished). 100 million members announced
(https://www.alltrails.com/press/alltrails-community-reaches-100-million-members);
more than one million subscribers in a separate case study
(https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/alltrails-product-channel). The two
figures are from different sources and dates and should not be divided into
a conversion rate.

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** Finding a hike in thirty
seconds. Brand, search presence, reviews, a land-manager programme, and a
partnership with REI.

**What they do worse.** Everything off the trail line: where to sleep, how
to drive there (their own help sends users to Google Maps:
https://support.alltrails.com/hc/en-us/articles/360055345532-Getting-Started-with-AllTrails-Overview),
whose rule applies. "Verified route" verifies the route.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Distribution. This is the company
whose search pages a newcomer has to get past to be seen at all.

**What we should not copy.** The word "verified" (already barred by the
trust principles), popularity signals, and tier reshuffles that move
features upward; the Peak launch is a live case study in resentment
(https://nz.trustpilot.com/review/alltrails.com).

**What their users still need another app for.** Camping, permits, road
status, water, and driving directions.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None. A casual hiker has no
reason to leave.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** "Where can I legally sleep
near this trailhead" for a Colorado weekend. It is a narrow add-on to a
habit they keep. AllTrails' own history says its backcountry users disliked
paying, which is the same segment.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

## CalTopo

Free; Mobile $20; Pro $50; Desktop $100 a year
(https://caltopo.com/about/pricing/individual-accounts/).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** Serious planning: terrain
analysis, printing, custom layers, team workspaces. And, notably, a public
table of each layer's source and update schedule
(https://help.caltopo.com/hc/en-us/articles/5379607151259-Map-Layer-Data-Updates),
which is the nearest thing any competitor has to Ohvernight's freshness
display.

**What they do worse.** It is a tool for people who already know what they
are doing. No discovery, no overnight question, no interpretation.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Technical depth and credibility
with search-and-rescue and professional users.

**What we should not copy.** The assumption of expertise. Nor its content:
the terms forbid scraping and commercial redistribution
(https://caltopo.com/about/terms-of-use).

**What their users still need another app for.** Rules, reservations,
casual discovery.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None. These users are not the
audience.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Little. A CalTopo user
reads forest orders unaided. Possibly the monitoring of them.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None. Worth studying as
proof that layer-level freshness can be shown plainly at $50 a year, by a
small company.

## Trailforks (Outside)

Pro $53.99 a year; Outside+ $89.99 (https://www.trailforks.com/pro/compare/).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** Trail networks maintained by
local associations, with conditions, reports and the ability to hide
sensitive or unsanctioned trails.

**What they do worse.** One activity family. Nothing about where to sleep.
Local curation says who edited a trail, not what the land manager permits.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** The relationships. Regional admins
are a network that cannot be bought or rebuilt quickly.

**What we should not copy.** Its data (API and attribution only:
https://www.trailforks.com/about/data/), and publishing a trail merely
because someone mapped it.

**What their users still need another app for.** Camping and every rule.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** A riding weekend with a
source-linked overnight attached. Small audience, strong habits, already
paying.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

## FarOut

Unlimited $15 a month, $72 per six months, $96 a year
(https://faroutguides.com/subscription/); individual lifetime guides also
sold. P1 could not verify the annual price; C8A could.

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** One long route, done
properly, offline, in partnership with the trail's own organisation
(including the Colorado Trail Foundation: https://faroutguides.com/).

**What they do worse.** Anything off its routes.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Guidebook depth and partner revenue
sharing on named routes.

**What we should not copy.** The purchased-guide unit; it does not fit a
question that starts with an activity. Do copy the idea that a route
organisation shares in the revenue.

**What their users still need another app for.** Every trip that is not a
through-route.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None; different job.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Nothing specific. Their
pricing is the useful part: a six-month season pass and lifetime purchases
show sellers in this market do not rely only on annual renewal.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

## COTREX (Colorado Parks and Wildlife)

Free according to B9 and P1 (https://cpw.state.co.us/cpw-apps); C8A found no
price stated on the pages it read and records it UNKNOWN. More than 225 land
managers (C8A, https://trails.colorado.gov/about) or more than 230 (B9); the
reports differ. Includes offline maps, advisories and route planning.

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** Colorado trails, from the
managers themselves, statewide, free, offline, with enforceable seasonal
closures. For trail facts in the pilot regions it is simply the better
product, and it has institutional standing Ohvernight cannot acquire.

**What they do worse.** Trails only. Its terms push legality back to the
user: depiction "is not ... an invitation to all types of travel"
(https://trails.colorado.gov/terms). No overnight workflow.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Authority and price. A state agency
giving the data away sets the price of Colorado trail information at zero.

**What we should not copy.** Literally: the terms prohibit copying and using
the data "to create or augment another dataset". M6 depends on permission
that has not been obtained.

**What their users still need another app for.** Somewhere to sleep, water
rules, bookings.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None, and it should not be the
aim. COTREX is the upstream.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** COTREX is free, so the
question is whether anyone pays Ohvernight at all once the trail half is
free and official. The overnight half has to carry the entire price.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None. The strategic question
is the opposite one: partner, licensee, or shut out.

# Part B — Camping and overnight (C8B)

## The Dyrt

PRO $59.99 a year with a 90-day trial on the checkout page
(https://support.thedyrt.com/hc/en-us/articles/360054724311-How-much-does-PRO-cost-What-is-the-renewal-cost,
https://thedyrt.com/pro/checkout/v4). Vehicle-dashboard integrations with
Jeep and Ram
(https://thedyrt.com/press/the-dyrt-named-to-inc-5000-for-fourth-consecutive-year-as-top-brands-recognize-the-leader-in-all-things-camping/).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** The whole visible overnight
product: national inventory, reviews, offline, trip planner, availability
alerts, discounts.

**What they do worse.** Its public-land layers show where camping "may" be
allowed
(https://support.thedyrt.com/hc/en-us/articles/360049651571-What-map-layers-does-The-Dyrt-PRO-have).
Reviewers complain of paying for free-camping information, alerts that found
nothing for weeks, and unclear Colorado entries
(https://apps.apple.com/us/app/the-dyrt-rv-free-camping/id1187626329?see-all=reviews&platform=watch).

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Breadth and distribution, including
inside the truck.

**What we should not copy.** Charging to see free-camping areas; a land
overlay that reads as permission; opaque auto-renewal (eight billing
complaints in the BBB's three-year summary:
https://www.bbb.org/us/or/portland/profile/camp/the-dyrt-1296-1000099660/complaints).

**What their users still need another app for.** The rule for the exact
spot, current orders, and what there is to do nearby.

**What feature could make a user switch.** For Colorado dispersed camping
specifically: restriction first, with the order and its dates, where The
Dyrt offers shading and a caveat. This is the closest thing to a genuine
switch on the list, and it depends entirely on claims Ohvernight has not
yet written.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Unlikely at similar prices.
A user choosing between two roughly $60 camping products keeps the one with
inventory. Ohvernight would have to be clearly cheaper, per-trip, or free.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** Regionally only: being the
trusted answer for Colorado overnight rules. Nationally, no.

## Hipcamp

Hosts pay 15% commission, 12.5% for integrated parks
(https://support.hipcamp.com/hc/en-us/articles/360024823412-How-much-does-it-cost-to-list-on-Hipcamp-and-is-there-a-commission-fee);
campers pay a variable fee, $3 minimum
(https://support.hipcamp.com/hc/en-us/articles/360025121211-What-is-the-Hipcamp-service-fee).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** A bookable private stay with
payment, host vetting and support. Permission here is unusually clean: the
landowner sold it to you.

**What they do worse.** Nothing about public land or what surrounds the
property. Host compliance with local rules is the host's problem.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Private supply and transactions. A
two-sided marketplace is not a one-person project.

**What we should not copy.** The marketplace. Also the incentive: a
commission business wants every listing to look bookable.

**What their users still need another app for.** Activities, public
alternatives, and rules beyond the property line.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None; Ohvernight has nothing to
book.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** None needed: Hipcamp is
free to browse. Ohvernight could send it traffic, which is an affiliate
question with a trust cost attached (see `business/business-model-options.md`).

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

## Campendium / Roadtrippers (Roadpass)

Roadtrippers $35.99, $49.99, $59.99 a year; Campendium RV PRO $59.99 a year
or $19.99 a month (https://roadtrippers.com/rv/,
https://campendium.com/membership/). More than 38 million trips planned
(https://roadpass.com/media/).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** The nearest existing thing
to an integrated planner: route, stops, RV-safe navigation, campgrounds,
overlays, an AI itinerary feature. It is the proof that integration can be
sold.

**What they do worse.** It is a road-trip product; recreation is a stop
type. Dispersed entries come with advice to contact the land manager
(https://support.campendium.com/hc/en-us/articles/4501226202388-What-is-dispersed-camping).
The merger of the two apps cost goodwill, including a discontinued Android
app (`hermes/q2-complaint-mining.md`, "What changed recently").

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Vehicle routing and the RV audience.

**What we should not copy.** Stop-count limits as the paywall; AI itineraries
over unreviewed data; folding one product into another and charging again.

**What their users still need another app for.** Tent and trail users, the
local rule, and activity depth.

**What feature could make a user switch.** For a Colorado weekend that is
not a road trip: starting from the activity, not the route. Different trip
shape more than a better product.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Weak. An RV traveller
crossing Colorado once will not add a regional subscription.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None. But this is the
competitor whose existence most undermines the claim that "nobody links
overnight to activities".

## iOverlander

Free with ads; Pro $59.99; Unlimited $99.99 a year
(https://ioverlander.com/subscriptions). Non-profit model; more than 300,000
traveller-submitted places (https://ioverlander.com/about); monthly prices
rose about 50% for new subscribers on 2026-01-01
(https://ioverlander.com/whats_coming).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** "Someone slept here last
week", worldwide and offline. Practical, recent, human.

**What they do worse.** That is all it says. Every place is a traveller's
report; legality is the reader's inference.

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Density of ground truth, with
contributors rewarded in free subscriptions.

**What we should not copy.** New user-submitted camping pins. The flywheel
study already excludes them
(`first-party-data-flywheel-options.md`, section 2.1).

**What their users still need another app for.** Whether it is allowed.

**What feature could make a user switch.** None; they would cross-check.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Cross-checking is
Ohvernight's most natural role here: the place to verify a pin found
elsewhere. People rarely pay for the second opinion.

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

## Recreation.gov

Free app; reservations generally carry an $8 service fee
(https://www.recreation.gov/reservation-policies). Fourteen federal agencies
(https://www.recreation.gov/faq).

**What they do better than Ohvernight today.** It is the authority and the
till for federal campgrounds: live inventory, policy text from the agency,
the booking itself. Ohvernight's campground layer is derived from its data.

**What they do worse.** Participating federal facilities only. No dispersed
camping, no state, county or private sites. It tells users to check agency

(https://www.recreation.gov/articles/list/plan-ahead-and-play-it-safe-for-your-next-outdoor-adventure/2259).

(https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.bah.r1smobile&hl=en_GB).

**What they would be hard to beat at.** Official transactions. Uncontestable.

**What we should not copy.** Nothing to copy. Do not imitate or intermediate
the booking.

**What their users still need another app for.** Everything that is not a
reservable federal site, and the surrounding trip.

**What feature could make a user switch.** For discovery only: a neutral map
across jurisdictions that hands off to Recreation.gov when a federal site is
the answer.

**What feature could make a user pay for both.** Recreation.gov is free. The
honest framing is dependency: RIDB access is revocable
(https://ridb.recreation.gov/access-agreement-ridb).

**What feature could eventually replace them.** None.

# Where incumbents are weak, and where a newcomer loses

## Structurally weak (reasons that are built into their business, not gaps they forgot)

| Weakness | Why it is structural | How durable |
|---|---|---|
| Permission is disclaimed, not answered | A national product cannot review rules place by place at a consumer price, and stating per-place permission invites liability. Every incumbent's terms push it back to the user | Durable while coverage is the selling point. Not a barrier if one of them decides Colorado is worth staffing |
| Organised by object, not by trip | onX by activity, Gaia by layer, AllTrails by trail, The Dyrt by campground, Recreation.gov by federal facility. Separate apps, separate bills | Organisational friction. Roadtrippers shows it can be crossed |
| Provenance at layer level only | Attaching source, date and authority to each statement means reviewing each statement | Same constraint as the first row |
| Crowd content ages and nobody expires it | Reviews and pins are engagement assets; deleting old ones shrinks the product | Could be changed tomorrow |
| Subscription behaviour | Bundles, tier reshuffles, auto-renewal. Resentment is documented across nearly all of them | Real, but resentment has not obviously cost them subscribers; no churn data exists in the reports |
| Colorado depth | National products are thin on county and district specifics | COTREX and CPW already have the depth, free |

INFERENCE: the weaknesses are real and they are all the same weakness seen
from different sides. Incumbents will not do slow, local, manual reading of
rules. That is exactly the work Ohvernight has chosen, and exactly the work
that does not scale for Ohvernight either.

## Where a newcomer simply loses

| Ground | Why |
|---|---|
| Offline maps and field navigation | Years of engineering, licensed tiles, and still the incumbents' top source of trip-ending complaints. Also given away free by COTREX, Google and Organic Maps |
| Inventory and reviews | Network effects measured in millions of contributors |
| Parcel and ownership data | Licensed, cleaned, national; a paid input for anyone else |
| Booking | Marketplaces and the federal system; payments, refunds, support |
| Availability alerts | A free specialist and two cheap paid ones already exist |
| Trail facts in Colorado | The state gives them away and forbids copying |
| Distribution | Search, app stores, dashboards in vehicles, media bundles |
| Price | Free government products set the floor for anything that looks like a map |
| Breadth | Any attempt at national or even statewide coverage meets the review-labour limit first |

The only ground not on that list is a reviewed, dated, source-linked answer
to the overnight question for specific Colorado places, plus the link to the
activity nearby. It is small, it is labour, and at present Ohvernight has not
occupied it.

## The three competitors that matter most

**1. COTREX (with CPW behind it).** Not because it is a rival but because it
is free, official, Colorado-specific and already trusted, and because M6
cannot proceed on its data without permission. It fixes the price of the
trail half of the thesis at zero and it decides whether Ohvernight is a
partner or an outsider. Every "why not just use the free state app?"
objection is about COTREX.

**2. onX.** The only incumbent already working the access question
seriously, already saying ownership is not access, already shipping
dispersed-camping data, and funded to go further. If claim-level permission
for Colorado ever proves commercially interesting, onX is who adds it. It is
also what Ohvernight's most plausible paying users already pay for, at up to
$99.99 a year.

**3. The Dyrt.** The incumbent a user would actually compare Ohvernight with
for an overnight decision. It defines what a camping product is expected to
include at $59.99 (inventory, offline, alerts, planner), and its documented
weak points (paid free-camping layers, "may allow" shading, failed alerts)
are the clearest opening on the list.

Left out deliberately. AllTrails matters for distribution, not for the
question Ohvernight asks. Recreation.gov is a dependency more than a
competitor. Roadtrippers is the best counter-example to "nobody integrates"
and worth watching for that reason, but it serves a different trip. Sēkr
matters as a precedent, not a competitor.
